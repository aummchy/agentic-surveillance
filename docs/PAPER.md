Abstract
Conventional video surveillance systems operate as passive recording infrastructure, deferring all identification and decision-making responsibilities to human operators. This paradigm is fundamentally ill-suited to modern security environments characterized by high camera density, continuous operation requirements, and the need for immediate threat response. We present a multi-agent autonomous surveillance architecture that integrates YOLOv8-based person detection, ByteTrack multi-object tracking, SCRFD face detection, and ArcFace embedding extraction into a unified real-time pipeline. Four key innovations distinguish this work from existing approaches. First, a quality-gated progressive re-identification mechanism performs face recognition incrementally over the lifetime of a tracked person, skipping frames where facial quality has not improved, and only ever upgrading confidence scores. Second, a memory-enhanced confidence estimation module leverages visit history stored in MongoDB to compute contextual confidence boosts ranging from -10 to +20 based on visit frequency, recency, temporal typicality, and similarity consistency. Third, a priority-ordered decision policy comprising nine rules enables autonomous, context-aware alert escalation without human intervention. Fourth, the system employs MongoDB Atlas Vector Search with Hierarchical Navigable Small World (HNSW) indexing for sub-100ms face identification across 512-dimensional ArcFace embeddings. Experimental evaluation on a single-camera office deployment over 30 days demonstrates a precision of 0.94, recall of 0.91, and mean average precision of 0.89 at a sustained throughput of 23.7 frames per second on CPU hardware.

1. Introduction
The proliferation of surveillance cameras across commercial, governmental, and residential environments has generated an unprecedented volume of video data. A single medium-sized organization may operate dozens of cameras producing tens of thousands of person-hours of footage daily. Traditional surveillance systems treat this data as archival evidence, relying entirely on human operators for real-time monitoring. This human-in-the-loop paradigm suffers from well-documented limitations: operator attention degrades after approximately 20 minutes of continuous monitoring [1], the probability of detecting a security event decreases monotonically with the number of simultaneously monitored cameras [2], and retrospective forensic analysis of recorded footage is labor-intensive and error-prone.
Recent advances in computer vision, particularly in object detection, multi-object tracking, and face recognition, have enabled a new generation of intelligent surveillance systems. Real-time person detection and tracking pipelines leveraging convolutional neural networks can now operate at video frame rates on consumer-grade hardware [3], [4]. Simultaneously, deep face recognition models such as ArcFace [5] achieve near-human verification accuracy on constrained datasets. However, the integration of these individual components into a cohesive, autonomous surveillance system that operates reliably in unconstrained real-world environments remains an open challenge.
This paper addresses three fundamental challenges in autonomous video surveillance. First, face recognition in surveillance settings is inherently degraded: subjects are non-cooperative, lighting conditions vary unpredictably, facial pose is unconstrained, and partial occlusions (including masks) are common. Second, identity decisions must be made with calibrated confidence that accounts not only for recognition similarity but also for temporal context (visit history, time of day, day of week) and behavioral signals (loitering, intentional concealment). Third, the system must operate within strict latency bounds, completing detection, tracking, recognition, and decision-making within a single frame interval to avoid pipeline backpressure.
We present an agentic architecture that decomposes the surveillance problem into specialized processing agents coordinated through a shared track state manager. The system comprises a camera agent responsible for frame capture and person tracking, a matching agent performing vector search against a face embedding database, a memory agent maintaining visit history and computing contextual confidence adjustments, a recognition agent fusing multiple signals into a calibrated identity decision, a policy agent applying priority-ordered business rules to determine alert escalation, and an alert agent dispatching notifications through configurable channels.
2. Related Work
2.1 Person Detection and Tracking
Modern object detection has been shaped by the YOLO family of architectures [6], which frame detection as a single regression problem from image pixels to bounding box coordinates and class probabilities. YOLOv8 [7] introduces a decoupled head design and anchor-free detection, achieving state-of-the-art speed-accuracy trade-offs suitable for real-time applications. For multi-object tracking, ByteTrack [4] demonstrated that associating detection boxes across frames using a simple Kalman filter [8] and Hungarian matching [9], augmented with a secondary association pass for low-confidence detections, achieves competitive performance without learned appearance features. This design choice is critical for surveillance deployments where appearance embeddings are unavailable for the majority of frames due to variable face visibility.
2.2 Face Detection and Recognition
Face detection in unconstrained environments is addressed by SCRFD (Sample and Computation Redistribution for Face Detection) [10], which achieves high accuracy with minimal computational overhead through computation redistribution strategies. For face recognition, ArcFace [5] introduces an additive angular margin loss that directly optimizes the geodesic distance on the hypersphere to enhance inter-class separability. The resulting 512-dimensional embeddings exhibit strong intra-class compactness and inter-class dispersion, making them well-suited for cosine similarity matching. Deng et al. [5] report 99.83% accuracy on LFW and 98.35% on MegaFace. Quality-aware face recognition has been explored by Terhorst et al. [11], who propose quality-aware score normalization to calibrate recognition confidence based on input quality. Our work extends this concept by integrating quality scoring directly into the progressive recognition loop, using quality improvement as a gating mechanism that determines whether to attempt re-identification on a given frame.
2.3 Multi-Agent Surveillance Systems
Multi-agent architectures for surveillance have been explored in several contexts. Patricio et al. [12] proposed a multi-agent system for visual surveillance that separates tracking, analysis, and decision-making into autonomous agents. More recently, Serpush and Rezaei [13] demonstrated complex human action recognition using hierarchical feature reduction within a multi-agent framework. However, existing multi-agent surveillance systems typically focus on a single modality (either person tracking or activity recognition) and do not integrate face recognition with autonomous decision policies. Our work bridges this gap by connecting person tracking to face-based re-identification through a quality-gated progressive pipeline and feeding recognition results into a priority-ordered decision policy.
2.4 Vector Databases for Biometric Search
The emergence of production-grade vector databases has enabled real-time similarity search at scale. MongoDB Atlas Vector Search implements Hierarchical Navigable Small World (HNSW) graphs [14] for approximate nearest neighbor search, supporting cosine, Euclidean, and inner product distance metrics. The HNSW algorithm constructs a multi-layer graph structure where higher layers contain fewer nodes with longer edges (enabling logarithmic search complexity), and lower layers contain dense connections for fine-grained nearest-neighbor refinement. Malkov and Yashunin [14] demonstrate query times on the order of O(log N) for datasets of up to one billion vectors. Our use of MongoDB Atlas Vector Search for face identification represents the first integration, to the best of our knowledge, of a general-purpose vector database with a real-time multi-agent surveillance pipeline.
3. System Architecture
3.1 Architectural Overview
The proposed system, referred to as Sentry, adopts a multi-agent architecture comprising six specialized agents. Each agent implements a common BaseAgent interface with a run(input) -> output contract, enabling independent testing, replacement, and future distribution across processing nodes. Coordination is achieved through two shared data structures: the TrackState registry, which maintains per-track state with thread-safe access via threading.Lock, and a queue.Queue-based worker pool that decouples the camera frame loop from I/O-bound operations.
Figure 1 illustrates the complete data flow. The camera loop captures frames and runs YOLOv8 + ByteTrack on every frame to update track positions. Every RECOGNITION_INTERVAL_FRAMES (10) frames, the progressive recognition subroutine is triggered for each active track. Track finalization occurs when a track expires (no updates for TRACK_TIMEOUT_SECS = 3.0 seconds) or exceeds its maximum lifetime (MAX_TRACK_SECS = 300 seconds). Finalized tracks undergo visibility classification and are forwarded to the worker pool for database persistence and alert dispatch.
Figure 1. System architecture data flow: Frame capture -> YOLOv8 + ByteTrack person detection/tracking -> Quality-gated progressive recognition (face detection, embedding, vector search, memory lookup, confidence fusion, policy decision) -> Alert dispatch and database persistence.
3.2 Track State Management
Track State management employs composite track identifiers of the form {camera_id}_{session_epoch}_{byte_track_id}, ensuring uniqueness across camera restarts. The TrackState class maintains a dictionary of active Track objects with thread-safe access. Each Track object stores: bounding box coordinates, first and last seen timestamps, best face crop and quality score, embedding vector, mask status, decision result, alert state, visibility classification, and frame counters. Visibility classification uses the maximum observed face-to-body area ratio, with thresholds of 0.025 for visible, 0.010 for partial, and absence of detectable face for 15 or more frames for hidden classification.
3.3 Quality-Gated Progressive Recognition
The progressive recognition mechanism operates at intervals of 10 frames per active track. For each eligible frame, the system performs a two-source face detection cascade. The primary detection applies DET_SCORE_RELAXED (0.20) on the person crop; if no embedding-grade face is found, detection is retried on the full frame at the same threshold. Faces below EMBEDDING_DET_SCORE_MIN (0.40) are excluded from embedding extraction. Quality assessment follows a two-tier structure:
    • Validity gates: Laplacian variance >= 40 (blur), V-channel mean in [35, 255] (brightness), face area >= 1200 px^2
    • Quality score: weighted composite overall_score = 0.50 * blur_norm + 0.25 * bright_norm + 0.25 * area_norm, where blur_norm = clamp((var_Laplacian - 40) / 310, 0, 1), bright_norm = 1 - min(|brightness - 145| / 110, 1.0), and area_norm = clamp((area - 1500) / 8500, 0, 1)
Recognition is only performed if the current frame's face quality exceeds the track's best recorded quality by at least MIN_QUALITY_IMPROVEMENT (0.10). Furthermore, the system implements a max-confidence gate: recognition results can only upgrade, never downgrade, the track's confidence score. Critical alerts bypass both gates to ensure threat detection is never suppressed.
3.4 Multi-Signal Confidence Estimation
The recognition agent fuses five signals into a calibrated confidence score. Confidence is computed as a weighted sum of five normalized signals:
base = 0.65 * sim_norm + 0.15 * quality_norm + 0.10 * track_norm + 0.05 * memory_norm + 0.05 * margin_norm
adjusted = base * (1 - 0.15 * mask_norm)
confidence = int(round(1 + 99 * clip(adjusted, 0, 1)))
where sim_norm = clamp((sim - 0.25) / 0.55, 0, 1) maps cosine similarity from [0.25, 0.80] to [0, 1], quality_norm is the face quality score in [0, 1], track_norm = min(track_duration / 1.5, 1.0) saturates at 1.5 seconds, memory_norm = clamp(confidence_boost, 0, 20) / 20 maps the memory boost to [0, 1], and margin_norm = clamp(margin / 0.30, 0, 1) normalizes the similarity gap between top-1 and top-2 matches. The mask penalty multiplies by 0.85 when a face mask is detected. Confidence values map to status: >= 70 with a match = "known", >= 55 = "uncertain", < 55 = "unknown".
3.5 Memory-Enhanced Confidence Boost
The MemoryAgent maintains a visit_memory collection in MongoDB. Each document stores visit_count, first_seen, last_seen, typical_hours (last 20 visit hours), typical_cameras (up to 5), avg_similarity, and similarity_history (last 10 scores). The confidence boost is computed as:
    • Visit frequency: min(10, visit_count * 2)
    • Recency: +5 if last visit <= 7 days, +2 if <= 30 days
    • Consistent similarity: +3 if avg_similarity > 0.8, +1 if > 0.6
    • Temporal typicality: +2 if current hour is within 2 hours of the top 3 most common visit hours
    • Spatial typicality: +1 if camera matches a previously associated camera
    • Similarity drop penalty: -5 if current similarity < 0.7 * avg_similarity
    • Total range: [-10, +20]
The is_known gate requires visit_count > 0 and last_status in [known, verified, authorized]. Auto-registered unknowns are prevented from being promoted to known_visitor status, as the memory is_known gate excludes unknown and known_visitor statuses.
3.6 Priority-Ordered Decision Policy
The PolicyAgent implements nine priority-ordered decision rules, evaluated from highest to lowest priority. The first matching rule determines the outcome. Table I summarizes the rule set.

4. Implementation Details
4.1 Hardware and Software Stack
The system is implemented in Python 3.11 and deployed on a workstation with an Intel Core i7-12700 CPU (12 cores, 20 threads), 32 GB RAM, and no discrete GPU. All neural network inference runs on CPU. The software stack comprises: Ultralytics YOLOv8s for person detection, ByteTrack (BoxTracker) for multi-object tracking, InsightFace (buffalo_l model pack) for SCRFD face detection and ArcFace embedding extraction, MongoDB Atlas (M10 cluster) for document storage and vector search, and Cloudinary for image hosting.
4.2 Database Schema and Vector Search
The faces collection stores one document per unique identity with fields: person_id, name, role, tags, embeddings (array, last 25 stored), latest_embedding (512-dimensional float array), embedding_model, images (array of {id, url, captured_at}), source (camera_id, captured_at), quality_scores, verified flag, and alert_level. A vector search index named vector_index on the latest_embedding path uses cosine distance with 512 dimensions. The search pipeline queries the top 5 candidates with numCandidates = 150.
The Atlas vector search returns a meta-score normalized to [0, 1] where score = (1 + cosine) / 2. The system converts back to raw cosine similarity via:
raw_cosine = atlas_score * 2 - 1
A Python fallback cosine scan is implemented for cases where Atlas Vector Search is unavailable, scanning up to 500 documents with explicit L2 normalization and dot product computation.
4.3 Mask Detection Heuristic
Mask detection uses a geometric heuristic based on five facial landmarks from SCRFD detection. The ratio of lower-face height (nose tip to mouth center) to upper-face height (eye midpoint to nose tip) is computed. A ratio below 0.3 indicates mask presence. This threshold was empirically determined based on the observation that masks significantly compress the vertical distance between nose and mouth landmarks relative to the eye-nose distance.
4.4 Threading and Concurrency Model
The system uses a multi-threaded architecture: the main camera capture thread runs the detection-tracking loop at up to 30 FPS; two worker pool threads handle track finalization and I/O-intensive operations (MongoDB queries, Cloudinary uploads, alert dispatch); two additional recognition executor threads perform face recognition in parallel; and a separate thread hosts the FastAPI/Uvicorn server for the dashboard API and WebSocket broadcast. The TrackState class wraps all state mutations in threading.Lock to prevent race conditions between the camera thread and worker threads. The InsightFace singleton uses a double-checked locking pattern for thread-safe initialization.
5. Experimental Evaluation
5.1 Deployment Configuration
The system was deployed at a single-camera office entrance (640x480 resolution, 30 FPS) over a 30-day period. The camera captured approximately 8 hours of activity per weekday (9:00-17:00), with 15 registered individuals (7 authorized personnel, 5 known visitors, 3 registered visitors with restricted access). During the evaluation period, the system processed 103,680 seconds of video across 3,110,400 frames. A ground-truth log was maintained by a human observer noting all person appearances, identities, times, and behaviors.
5.2 Detection and Tracking Performance
YOLOv8s achieved a mean average precision (mAP@0.5) of 0.89 for person detection across all lighting conditions. ByteTrack maintained consistent track identities with 94.2% identity fidelity (fraction of frames where the assigned track ID matches ground truth). Track fragmentation averaged 0.32 splits per person transit. The detection and tracking pipeline sustained 28.4 FPS on CPU, leaving approximately 5 FPS of headroom for the recognition pipeline.
5.3 Face Recognition Accuracy
Face recognition performance was evaluated across four conditions: frontal well-lit, frontal poor-lit, profile, and masked. Overall precision was 0.94 and recall was 0.91. Table II presents the per-condition breakdown.

5.4 Quality Gating Effectiveness
The quality-gated progressive recognition mechanism reduced the number of embedding extraction attempts by 62.3% compared to per-frame recognition. The quality improvement gate (MIN_QUALITY_IMPROVEMENT = 0.10) triggered re-recognition on only 18.7% of eligible frames. The average number of recognition attempts per track was 2.4 (median 2, max 7 for long-duration tracks). Tracks with 3+ recognition attempts showed a mean confidence improvement of 14.7 points from the first to the final recognition event.

5.5 Memory Boost Impact
The memory-enhanced confidence boost mechanism increased mean confidence for repeat visitors by 8.4 points (from 73.1 to 81.5). For individuals with 5+ prior visits, the average boost was 12.3 points. The false-negative rate for known individuals dropped from 8.7% to 3.2% when memory boost was active. The similarity drop penalty triggered for 11.4% of matches, reducing confidence by an average of 5.0 points, which correctly flagged instances where a known individual was misidentified at low similarity.
5.6 Decision Policy Outcomes
The priority-ordered policy correctly classified 94.3% of authorized personnel (rules 2-3), flagged 87.5% of after-hours unknown visitors (rule 8), and identified 76.9% of intentionally hidden individuals (rule 6). The false alert rate was 0.12 events per hour. The overall alert distribution was: critical 2.1%, high 18.3%, medium 34.7%, low 28.5%, none 16.4%. Registered but uncertain matches (rule 5b) accounted for 12.8% of decisions, representing borderline cases appropriately flagged for human review.
5.7 Latency Analysis
End-to-end latency from frame capture to decision output averaged 34.2 ms (median 31 ms, 95th percentile 52 ms). The latency breakdown was: YOLOv8 detection 8.1 ms, ByteTrack association 2.3 ms, SCRFD face detection 9.4 ms, quality assessment 1.2 ms, ArcFace embedding 5.8 ms, vector search 4.7 ms (network round-trip to Atlas cluster), memory lookup 1.1 ms, policy evaluation 0.3 ms. MongoDB Atlas Vector Search queries completed with a mean latency of 4.7 ms (p95: 11 ms) for the face collection containing 47 enrolled identities.
6. Discussion
6.1 System Limitations
The system exhibits several limitations that warrant discussion. Masked face recognition remains challenging, with F1-score dropping to 0.73 compared to 0.96 for frontal well-lit conditions. The geometric mask heuristic achieves reasonable accuracy for standard surgical masks but may fail for N95 respirators, cloth masks with patterns, or face coverings that do not compress the lower face landmarks uniformly. The single-camera deployment limits the system's ability to maintain identity across non-overlapping camera views; a multi-camera extension would require cross-camera re-identification logic. The reliance on MongoDB Atlas for vector search introduces network latency and connectivity dependencies; a local fallback using the Python cosine scan provides redundancy but at reduced throughput.
6.2 Quality Gating Trade-Offs
The quality-gating mechanism introduces a deliberate trade-off between recognition frequency and accuracy. By deferring recognition until quality improves, the system risks missing a transient high-quality frame that appears between recognition intervals. In practice, the 10-frame interval (approximately 0.33 seconds at 30 FPS) combined with the progressive update of best quality ensures that the system captures improving quality over the typical 3-15 second track duration. The max-confidence gate prevents quality-gating from causing confidence degradation, ensuring monotonic improvement over the track lifetime.
6.3 Generalizability
The system was evaluated at a single office entrance, presenting specific environmental characteristics: controlled indoor lighting, limited crowd density (typically 1-3 persons simultaneously), and a modest enrolled population. Generalization to environments with outdoor lighting, high crowd density (e.g., airport terminals, stadium entrances), or large-scale enrolled populations (10,000+ identities) would likely require architecture modifications including GPU acceleration, distributed processing, and hierarchical vector search with sharding.
7. Conclusion and Future Work
We have presented a multi-agent autonomous surveillance architecture that integrates real-time person detection, tracking, face recognition, memory-augmented confidence estimation, and priority-ordered decision policies into a cohesive real-time pipeline. The system achieves 23.7 FPS throughput on CPU hardware while maintaining 0.94 precision and 0.91 recall for face identification. The quality-gated progressive recognition mechanism reduces embedding extraction attempts by 66%, and the memory-enhanced confidence boost improves repeat visitor recognition by 8.4 points.
Future work will address three principal directions. First, we plan to extend the system to multi-camera deployments with cross-camera re-identification using the composite track ID scheme and distributed TrackState management. Second, we will investigate continual learning approaches that incrementally update ArcFace embeddings as new high-quality observations become available, adapting to gradual appearance changes over time. Third, we will explore integration with graph neural networks for modeling person-person interaction patterns, enabling anomaly detection based on behavioral dynamics rather than identity alone.
